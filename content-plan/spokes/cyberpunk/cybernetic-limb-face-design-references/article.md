Bran emailed me her brief on a Sunday in November. The character was a Maelstrom-affiliated solo named Kestrel, and the line of the brief I kept coming back to was: "She has more chrome than face. I want it to feel sad, not cool." That sentence reorganised the whole portrait. The painting wasn't going to be a chrome-out flex piece. It was going to be a portrait of someone who had traded most of herself away.

Painting cyberware is the part of cyberpunk character work that gets the most wrong, and not because painters can't render chrome. It's because most cyberware in fiction has no design language behind it — it's just "shiny metal stuck on the body." Real prosthetic design, real medical implants, real industrial machinery — those things have visual rules, manufacturing constraints, and signs of use that distinguish them from each other. A character art commission lives or dies on whether the chrome reads as built by someone, owned by someone, and worn for a reason.

## Three categories

When I paint cyberware, I sort every piece into one of three categories before I render a single highlight. The category determines how the part is shaped, what it's made of, and how it sits against the skin.

**Industrial** cyberware is mass-produced, function-first, and ugly. Visible welds. Exposed bolts. Hydraulic lines that run on the outside of the limb because the manufacturer didn't bother to hide them. The colour palette is brushed steel, oxidised brass, military matte. A Militech-issued combat prosthetic is industrial. A Maelstrom street-bought arm welded onto someone in a back-alley clinic is industrial-with-modifications, which is its own visual register.

**Medical** cyberware is the opposite end. Designed to disappear. Smooth seams, neutral matte finishes, careful colour-matching to the skin around it. The wear pattern is gentle — these pieces are owned by people who can afford regular cleanings and tune-ups. A Trauma Team-issue spinal jack on a corporate exec is medical. A high-end Kiroshi optic on a fixer who pays for upkeep is medical. The colour is bone-white, surgical grey, brushed titanium with no exposed mechanism.

**Designer** cyberware is the third category and the one cyberpunk illustration usually misses. It's medical-grade engineering with deliberate aesthetic choices on top. Engraving. Lacquer paint. Decorative inlay. Visible serial numbers as a fashion statement rather than a manufacturer obligation. This is what an Arasaka exec wears in 2077, what a wealthy Tyger Claw boss wears in either era, what a Voodoo Boys senior netrunner has commissioned by their own diaspora's craftspeople. The colour can be anything — black chrome, rose gold, etched bronze — and it carries personality the way couture clothing does.

Most characters have cyberware from one category. The interesting ones have pieces from two. A solo who runs an industrial Militech combat arm and a designer Kiroshi optic is telling you a story: she does her work cheap and her seeing expensive. Same character, two registers, the painting reads richer.

## Limb prosthetics at portrait scale

A portrait usually shows a chest-up or three-quarter view, which means the cyberware you can show is hands, forearms, and shoulders — not full legs and not the full mechanical workings of anything. The detail you paint has to land in that frame.

For a **forearm prosthesis**, what reads at portrait scale:

- The seam where the prosthesis meets the flesh. Is it clean (medical), welded with visible scarring (industrial), or hidden under a decorative cuff (designer)?
- The plating pattern. How is the limb segmented? Industrial limbs are usually four to six large plates, designer limbs run twenty or more smaller plates for articulation, medical limbs hide segmentation entirely under a continuous skin-toned shell.
- One distinctive surface detail. A maker's mark. A scratch from a fight. A rust patch. A sticker the character has put on. The one detail that says "this limb has a history."

A full **chrome arm** in a portrait is mostly silhouette and finish. I usually paint the chrome with about three value steps — a deep shadow that reads almost as Ink, a mid-tone that picks up ambient colour, and a sharp specular highlight that catches the warm key light. Mirror-perfect chrome is a beginner mistake; real chrome on a working limb is scratched, dulled in spots, and reflects an abstracted version of the environment, not a photographic one.

For a **hand prosthesis**, the joints carry the read. Painting cyberware hands is mostly about painting how the fingers articulate — where the visible hinges are, how the knuckle plates overlap, whether the fingertips are sensor-tipped or just blunt metal. The hand is also where signs-of-use show up most clearly, because a working hand picks up scuffs and oil that a forearm doesn't.

## Face plates, cyberoptics, sub-dermal work

The face is the highest-stakes part of any portrait, and cyberware on the face is the highest-stakes part of the highest-stakes part. Get it wrong and the painting reads as a costume mask. Get it right and the character becomes unforgettable.

**Cyberoptics** are the most common face cyberware in commissions. There are three sub-types worth knowing:

- The **Kiroshi-style optic** is medical-grade, smooth, with a visible ring or iris pattern that replaces the natural iris. The sclera (white of the eye) stays human. The change is contained to the iris and pupil, which carry a slight chrome ring and an unnatural-but-not-glowing colour — desaturated amber, cold blue, soft jade. These read as "augmented person" rather than "person with robot eye."
- The **full-replacement chrome eye** is industrial. The whole eye socket is a chrome assembly with a central aperture that opens and closes like a camera shutter. The sclera is gone, replaced by polished metal. These read aggressive — the eye is no longer a window, it's a sensor. Maelstrom characters lean into this.
- The **disguised optic** is designer. A natural-looking eye with a single small tell — a faint chromatic ring at the iris edge, an unnatural sharpness to the highlight, a pupil that doesn't quite respond to the light in the painting the way a human one would. The viewer doesn't immediately register the cyberware. They just feel something is off.

> Glow is not a substitute for design. A pair of glowing eyes is the cheapest, laziest cyberware signifier in the genre, and it is almost always wrong unless the character is mid-jack or mid-combat-mode.

I will paint glow when the brief specifically calls for an active mode — a netrunner connected to the Net, a solo with combat reflex on, a hacker mid-intrusion. Otherwise the eye stays passive and the cyberware reads as ambient presence, not as a light source.

**Face plates** — partial or full chrome covering some of the skull — are the most cyberpunk-iconic face cyberware and the easiest to wreck. The rule I use: a face plate should follow the underlying skull anatomy, not contradict it. The cheekbone should still read as a cheekbone, even if it's chrome. The jaw should hinge where a jaw hinges. The plate should look like it was fitted to this specific skull, not stamped from a generic mould.

**Sub-dermal cyberware** is the subtlest and one of my favourites to paint. The implant sits under the skin, and what shows on the surface is just a faint relief — a line, a port, a slightly different skin texture along the edge. Sub-dermal work is what corporate execs and high-end fixers wear, because it does the function without announcing it. In a portrait, sub-dermal work is mostly a single thin highlight along the underlying shape, and a slight discolouration around the seam.

## The tasteful augmentation vs chrome-out spectrum

Every cyberware brief lives on a spectrum from "augmented person" to "chrome-out post-human." Where the character sits on that spectrum is one of the first conversations I have with the client, because it determines the entire palette and silhouette of the portrait.

A few rough anchors on the spectrum:

- **Tasteful augmentation (low chrome):** one or two pieces, mostly hidden or designed to integrate. The character reads as human first, augmented second. Most corpos, most fixers, most medtechs sit here.
- **Visible augmentation (medium chrome):** three to five pieces, at least one prominent. The character reads as cyberpunk genre at a glance. Most player characters sit here.
- **Chromed-up (high chrome):** six or more pieces, including face work. The character reads as augmented first, human second. Senior gang members, certain solos, some rockerboys sit here.
- **Cyberpsycho-adjacent:** the character has more cyberware than baseline humanity. The painting needs to acknowledge that this is dangerous. The skin under the chrome should look unhappy — pallor, vein discolouration, signs that the body is rejecting the metal it's hosting. This is a painting register I'll only take if the brief asks for it specifically, because it's a hard piece to make beautiful.

Bran's brief, the one I opened this piece with, was a chrome-out portrait that needed to read as sad rather than as cool. The trick I used: I painted Kestrel's remaining human skin with cool, slightly desaturated tones — not warm, not healthy — and I let the chrome carry the warm light. The chrome ended up looking more alive than the woman wearing it. That inversion was the whole painting. Sometimes the cyberware is the figure and the person is the negative space.

## Rendering chrome

A short technical note, because chrome is where most painters fall down. Chrome is not a colour. Chrome is a behaviour — it reflects everything around it, distorted by the curvature of its surface. A chrome forearm in a dark alley should reflect the dark alley, plus the warm key light, plus whatever neon is in the scene. A chrome forearm in a clean medical bay reflects bone-white walls and overhead fluorescents.

The three chrome finishes I paint most often:

- **Brushed brass** — warm-toned, slightly matte, with horizontal striations along the surface that catch light at a narrower angle than mirror chrome would. This is my default for working-class industrial cyberware. It reads warm, period, and worn.
- **Oxidised mirror chrome** — cooler-toned, mostly reflective with patches of dull green-grey corrosion where the surface has been chipped or scratched. The corrosion is what makes the surface believable. Brand-new chrome reads as plastic. Slightly aged chrome reads as metal.
- **Black chrome** — the designer-end finish, deep Ink-dark with sharp Parchment-white speculars and a slight iridescent shift where the surface curves. This is what high-end couture cyberware looks like, and it's the hardest to render because the value range is so compressed.

I paint chrome in three passes: a flat mid-tone base in the chrome's "colour," then a darker shadow pass that reads almost Ink in the deepest crevices, then a final pass for the highlights — small, sharp, placed deliberately along the form. The temptation is to over-render the highlights. Resist it. Three or four well-placed sharp speculars on a forearm read as chrome. Twenty highlights read as a Christmas ornament.

## Signs of use

Every piece of cyberware should have at least one sign of use, because new-looking cyberware reads as concept art rather than as a portrait of a working person.

Things I paint into the chrome:

- A scratch along one plate edge, deeper than the surrounding wear, from a specific incident the character could tell you about.
- A patch of dulled finish where the character's hand grips the prosthesis regularly.
- A residue line — oil, grease, dried blood, sweat salt — along a seam.
- A maker's mark or serial number, partially worn off, in a place that suggests the limb has been re-finished at least once.
- A piece of personalisation: a sticker, an engraving, a small charm welded to the chrome by the character themselves.

One sign of use per piece, sometimes two. More than that and the painting starts to read as a damage map rather than as a character. The signs should feel deliberate, like they each came from one specific moment.

## Common mistakes that flatten the piece

A short list of what I quietly correct on most cyberware briefs:

- **Symmetrical chrome.** Real prosthetics are not symmetrical to each other or to the body they replace. A left arm prosthesis is not a mirror of the right arm — it's a slightly different model, installed at a different time, with different wear.
- **Floating chrome.** Cyberware that doesn't seem connected to the body around it. There should always be a visible seam, a transition zone, or a deliberate fashion choice (a glove, a sleeve) hiding the connection.
- **Identical finish across all pieces.** A character with chrome on the arm, the eye, and the temple should not have all three rendered in the same chrome. Different ages, different manufacturers, different finishes. The mismatch is the realism.
- **No skin reaction.** Chrome that sits against flesh should have some effect on that flesh — slight discolouration along the seam, a healed scar, occasionally a patch of irritation. Skin doesn't accept metal silently.
- **Over-symmetrical face work.** A half-face plate that perfectly bisects the face is a cliché. Real surgical work follows anatomy, not symmetry. The plate should cover the parts of the face that needed covering, not a clean half.

## How to brief cyberware

The brief format that works best:

1. Hero piece — one specific implant, named by type and rough manufacturer if you have one.
2. Functional piece — one supporting implant, can be implied.
3. Personality piece — one cosmetic or aesthetic choice.
4. The category of each (industrial / medical / designer).
5. The overall position on the chrome spectrum (tasteful / visible / chromed-up / cyberpsycho-adjacent).
6. One sign of use per piece, if you have an idea — otherwise I'll invent them.
