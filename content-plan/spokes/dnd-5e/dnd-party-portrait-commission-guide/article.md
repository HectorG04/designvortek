The single most common mistake in a party portrait brief is the line "everyone facing the camera, smiling." That brief produces a yearbook. I've painted six of those and they all look the same — five characters lined up on a stage, weight evenly distributed, the eye bouncing back and forth with nowhere to settle. A party portrait is a *composition*, not a class photo, and the brief has to treat it that way.

This is a guide for players and GMs commissioning a four-to-six character group portrait. It covers height hierarchy, posture variety, color-coding so each character reads as themselves, the geometry of group composition, and the failure modes I see every month at the studio. If you've got a Tuesday-night party who's been together for two years and the talk has finally turned to a group painting, this is the file to read before you write the brief.

## Why the yearbook fails

A party portrait fails when every character is doing the same thing with their body. Five people standing, all facing front, all weight on both feet, all looking at the viewer. There's no compositional rhythm. The eye reads left to right, then leaves the painting. Nothing pulls it back. The painting has no *center of gravity*.

The fix isn't to make one character the hero of the painting — that produces resentment from the other players and a portrait that doesn't represent the party. The fix is to give each character a *different posture verb*. One leans. One sits. One stands and looks elsewhere. One crouches. One stands behind the others. Now there's hierarchy without favoritism, and the eye has a path through the scene.

A six-person party I painted last spring — Aurelia campaign, two-year run, the group had finally beaten the campaign arc and wanted a wall piece — came to me with a brief that said "everyone in their hero pose, all looking forward." I sent back three thumbnail compositions, none of which matched the brief, and asked them to pick which one they could *live with*. They picked the third one: their cleric kneeling at the front, examining a fallen banner; their fighter behind the cleric, hand on the cleric's shoulder; their warlock standing apart, looking up at something the viewer can't see; their bard mid-laugh at the warlock; their rogue half-out of the composition on the right, already moving; their wizard at the back, writing in a journal. Six characters. Six verbs. The painting reads.

## Height hierarchy and the eye-line problem

A common impulse is to line characters up by height — tallest on the ends, shortest in the middle, or vice versa. This is exactly the move that creates the yearbook problem. Real groups don't pose by height. They stand where they stand.

What matters for a party portrait isn't who's tallest in real-stats terms. What matters is the *eye-line stagger* — where each character's gaze ends up on the canvas. If three characters' eye-lines are at the same horizontal axis, the painting reads as a row. If the eye-lines stagger — one high, one low, one mid, one looking down — the eye has a vertical path through the painting and the composition gets depth.

To brief this, don't list heights. List *vertical anchors*. For each character, say where in the frame their face should sit — top-third, mid-third, lower-third, or off-frame at the edge. Six anchors across a wide canvas, no two at the same height, and the height problem solves itself. ## Posture variety: the bored-versus-engaged test

Here's a quick test for any party portrait reference: imagine each character in isolation, removed from the group, with a neutral grey background. Are they doing anything? Or are they standing? If three or more characters wouldn't be doing *anything* without the rest of the party there, the composition is hollow.

A few posture verbs that consistently produce strong character moments in a group:

- **Examining** — kneeling, hand reaching for something, attention on an object rather than the viewer
- **Resting** — sitting on a step, on a barrel, on a fallen log, weight on the hands rather than the legs
- **Pivoting** — caught mid-turn, weight on one foot, the other foot lifting, eye-line ahead of the body
- **Speaking** — mouth open, hand mid-gesture, listener implied even if not in frame
- **Watching** — eye-line off-canvas, body still, the character noticing something the others haven't yet
- **Carrying** — load on the back, on the shoulder, in the arms, body posture serving the burden
- **Reading** — head down to a book or scroll or letter, hands occupied, world muted
- **Sleeping** — yes, including a sleeping character in a party portrait works astonishingly well, particularly for a party that travels together

Two characters can share a verb if they're *interacting* — both speaking, both reading the same map, both pivoting in the same direction. But three characters doing the same thing is a flag the composition isn't working yet. ## Color-coding without costumes

Every character in a party portrait needs to be *readable as themselves* at any zoom. The way to do that without making the painting look like a Power Rangers lineup is to assign each character a *color signature* — one dominant hue plus a single accent. Not a costume in the color. A garment, a piece of leather, a tattoo, a lining inside a hood — something that signals the character at a glance.

The trick is choosing the signature colors *together*, as a palette, rather than each player picking a favorite. A palette of six saturated jewel tones — emerald, sapphire, ruby, amber, violet, citrine — fights itself across a single canvas. A palette built around an Ink-and-Gold neutral with three accent colors plus three earth tones reads as a *party that traveled together long enough to start looking related*.

A working pattern for a six-character party:

- **Two characters in earth tones** with one accent each — the rogue and the ranger, for instance, both in browns and dark greens, with one a deep wine accent at the throat and the other a bone-pale wrap at the wrist
- **One character in a saturated jewel tone** — the bard in a singular sapphire-blue, drawing the eye to the social anchor of the group
- **One character in pure tonal contrast** — the wizard in a muted parchment-cream that reads against everyone else's darks
- **Two characters with metallic signatures** — the paladin and the fighter, one steel-cool and one warm bronze, the metal doing the work the color usually does

This logic is the same one the [fantasy color palette and faction warmth](/blog/fantasy-color-palette-faction-warmth) breakdown explores for solo characters, scaled up to a group. Color discipline at the brief stage is what saves the painting from chaos at the polish stage.

## The four composition shapes

There are four reliable shapes a party of four-to-six characters can fit into. Picking the shape before writing the rest of the brief is the single biggest unlock.

### The V-composition

Characters arranged in a wide V, with one or two at the apex (closer to the viewer, lower in the frame) and the rest spreading up and back. Great for parties with a clear point-character at the front — a paladin leading, a cleric examining something, a wizard reading a map. Eye lands on the apex, then sweeps up either side.

### The line composition

A horizontal arrangement across the canvas, but *with verticals broken up*. One character sitting, one standing, one half-turned, one cropped at the edge. Works for tavern-bench scenes, council-room scenes, or any moment where the party is at rest. The eye reads left to right but the staggered verticals slow the read.

### The cluster composition

A tight ring of characters with no clear front-rank, often three to four characters huddled around a center object — a map, a fire, a body. The eye is drawn inward by the cluster geometry. Best for emotional beats. A four-person party portrait I painted last winter used this shape: the four characters were gathered around the body of an NPC they hadn't been able to save. No one was looking at the viewer. The painting was about the silence between them.

### The diagonal composition

Characters arranged along a strong diagonal axis — one at the lower-left, climbing up and to the right. Often works for parties in motion or parties with a clear narrative direction. A ranger-and-druid-led wilderness party reads well in this shape. A diagonal also gives you natural depth: front-character close, back-character far.

> A party portrait isn't six portraits side by side. It's one painting that happens to contain six people.

## The brief, line by line

A good party portrait brief has a specific architecture. Here's what to include and the order to include it in:

1. **The campaign one-liner**. One sentence about the party's arc. "We've been running Curse of Strahd for fourteen months and we just got out of Castle Ravenloft alive." This gives the painter tone.
2. **The composition shape**. V, line, cluster, or diagonal — pick one and commit. If you can't pick, ask the painter for thumbnails before the brief is finalized.
3. **The setting**. Where is this portrait happening? A specific place in your campaign world is always better than a generic backdrop. Tavern interior at dusk. Forest clearing at dawn. Mountain pass at midday. Throne room after the coronation. The setting determines the light, which determines everything else.
4. **Each character, in eye-line order**. For each character, give the painter: name, species/subspecies, class, vertical anchor (top-third, mid-third, lower-third), posture verb, color signature, one distinctive accessory or feature. That's it. Seven data points per character. A six-person party brief is forty-two data points plus the four pieces of group context. Pages of backstory don't help.
5. **The emotional read**. One line. "Triumph but tired." "Quiet between battles." "Just before the door opens." This is the most important line in the brief.
6. **Three to five mood references**. Pinterest board, film stills, paintings. Not "draw this exactly" — references for light, color, group composition energy.

## What gets lost in a six-character portrait

A six-character canvas has roughly the same total face-area as a one-character canvas of the same dimensions. Each character gets less detail. A few things I quietly de-emphasize at the briefing stage so I don't have to later:

- **Eye color, on the smaller characters in the composition**. At the size each face will print, eye color reads more as value than hue. A pale eye reads pale; a dark eye reads dark. The actual color barely registers on the back-rank characters.
- **Small jewelry**. Earrings, finger rings, smaller pendants — if they're not at least a fingernail's width on the canvas, they read as noise rather than detail.
- **Pattern detail on fabric**. Embroidery, complex stitching, faction insignia — these compress to fuzz at typical party-portrait scale. Solid color blocks with edge wear read better than fine pattern work.
- **Background characters' weapons**. The fighter at the back doesn't need her sword fully visible. The hilt or the pommel implies the rest.

What does survive at any scale: silhouette, posture, color signature, gesture, and the *direction the face is turned*. Brief hard for those five things, brief lightly for everything else, and the painting will hold together.
